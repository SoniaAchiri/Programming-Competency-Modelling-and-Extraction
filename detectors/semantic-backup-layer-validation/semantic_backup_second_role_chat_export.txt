Genuinely-wrong submissions are systematically more similar to the
correct exemplars than the novel-correct submissions are. And this isn't
noise — std=0.039 on the AUC across 10 different exemplar draws is
tight, meaning this reversal shows up no matter which 8 exemplars get
picked. This is a real, stable, strongly-reversed finding, not an
inconclusive one.

Why this makes sense, once you think about it — and this connects
directly to something the paper already established (§5.1): most
wrong-labeled submissions in this dataset aren't wrong because they
attempted something different — they correctly implement the standard,
typical pattern and fail on an unrelated edge case. That means they
look, syntactically, very close to a "textbook" correct exemplar.
Meanwhile novel_correct submissions are, by definition, doing something
atypical — that's the entire reason the AST rules didn't recognize them.
So of course they read as less similar to a registry built from typical
correct examples — their whole defining trait is that they don't look
typical, even though they behave correctly.

What this means for the layer's actual design, stated in Section 7: the
core assumption — "a high similarity score means this looks like it's
doing the same thing as a correct reference solution" — doesn't hold
here. As built, high similarity-to-exemplar is actually a better signal
for "attempted the standard approach" (which correlates with the typical
near-miss wrong submissions) than for "is correct." If you deployed this
exact mechanism as-is, it would tend to flag the wrong population for
review.

This is a genuinely more valuable result than a null one — it's not "the
layer doesn't work," it's "the layer's specific decision rule (raw
max-similarity-to-correct-exemplars) is measuring something other than
what it needs to." A concrete next step worth trying: instead of
similarity-to-correct-exemplars alone, build a second registry from
typical wrong submissions and use the difference (or ratio) between
similarity-to-correct and similarity-to-wrong as the signal, rather than
similarity-to-correct in isolation — that could plausibly separate
"close to correct, but also close to typical-wrong" (ambiguous) from
"close to correct, and not close to typical-wrong" (a better candidate
for genuine novel-correct).
